The one idea to keep straight all unit

Short run vs. long run. In the short run, policy can change real GDP and unemployment. In the long run, the economy returns to potential output and the natural rate of unemployment, so demand-side policy only changes the price level or inflation. The only way to raise output in the long run is to grow potential output itself (LRAS shifts right). Almost every Unit 5 question is testing which of these two worlds you’re in.

Module 5.1

Fiscal and monetary policy actions in the short run

Fiscal policy (government spending and taxes) and monetary policy (the central bank’s interest-rate tools) both shift aggregate demand. They can be used separately, or together, to close a recessionary or inflationary gap. When they’re combined, you have to think about what each one does to AD and to the interest rate.

🧸 Explained simply

The economy has two steering wheels. One belongs to the government (spend more or less, tax more or less). The other belongs to the central bank (make borrowing cheaper or more expensive). Both can speed the economy up or slow it down.

If both wheels turn the same way, you know for sure where the car goes. If they turn opposite ways, you can only be sure about some things.

Words, made simple

Recessionary gap
The economy is making less than it could. Too many people without jobs.
Inflationary gap
The economy is running too hot. Prices are climbing fast.
Expansionary policy
Hitting the gas: spend more, tax less, or make borrowing cheaper.
Contractionary policy
Hitting the brakes: spend less, tax more, or make borrowing more expensive.
Dual mandate
The Fed’s two jobs from Congress: lots of jobs and steady prices.

The independence of fiscal and monetary policy

Central banks usually play no role in fiscal policy, and in many countries governments play no role in monetary policy. Congress gave the Federal Reserve a dual mandate: pursue maximum employment and price stability. Beyond those broad goals, Congress decided that monetary policy should be free from political influence, so the Fed is an independent agency.

Closing gaps with each tool

Expansionary fiscal: G ↑ or T ↓→AD ↑→real GDP ↑, PL ↑, unemployment ↓
Expansionary monetary: buy bonds→MS ↑→i ↓→I and C ↑→AD ↑

Combining the two: what is certain and what isn’t

When both policies push AD the same way, the effect on AD is certain, but their effects on the interest rate fight each other. When they push AD in opposite directions, the effect on AD is uncertain, but their effects on the interest rate line up.

CombinationEffect on AD (real GDP, PL)Effect on interest rate

Why mix them?

A government that runs a big deficit in a recession pushes interest rates up and risks crowding out private investment (Module 5.5). If the central bank also expands the money supply at the same time, it can hold interest rates down so that the fiscal stimulus has its full effect on AD.

Two kinds of policy, two decision-makers

Monetary policy is the action of central banks to achieve goals such as price stability, full employment, and economic growth. Fiscal policy is the tax and spending policy of the government, set when it determines its budget. Central banks generally don’t play any role in fiscal policy.

The Fed’s dual mandate

Congress gave the Fed the mandate to pursue maximum employment and price stability, and decided monetary policy should be free from political influence. As a result, the Federal Reserve is an independent agency.

Check yourself

The economy is in a recession. Name one fiscal and one monetary policy that would close the gap.
Fiscal: increase government purchases, cut taxes, or raise transfers. Monetary: buy government bonds (or lower the IORB) to reduce interest rates. Both shift AD right.
The government increases spending while the Fed sells bonds. What happens to the interest rate and to real GDP?
The interest rate rises for sure (both push it up). The effect on real GDP is uncertain: fiscal policy shifts AD right, monetary policy shifts it left, and we don’t know which is bigger.
Why did Congress make the Fed independent?
So that monetary policy decisions, like raising interest rates to fight inflation, aren’t blocked by short-term political pressure.

Module 5.2

The Phillips curve

In the short run, lower unemployment comes with higher inflation, and higher unemployment comes with lower inflation. That trade-off is the short-run Phillips curve. In the long run there is no trade-off: the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.

🧸 Explained simply

When the economy is busy, almost everyone has a job, stores are packed, and prices go up fast. When the economy is slow, lots of people lose jobs and prices barely rise. So in the short run, fewer jobless people = faster-rising prices. That’s a seesaw, and the short-run Phillips curve draws it.

But if the government keeps pushing for super-low unemployment, people start expecting prices to rise, and they ask for bigger raises. Then prices rise even faster, and the seesaw itself slides up. In the long run, unemployment goes back to its normal level no matter what, and the only thing you changed is how fast prices are rising.

Words, made simple

Output gap
How far the economy is from what it could normally make. Negative = slow. Positive = too hot.
Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC)
A downhill line: low unemployment goes with high inflation, and the other way around.
Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)
A straight-up line at the normal unemployment rate. In the long run, you always end up there.
Expected inflation
How fast people think prices will rise. If they expect more, they ask for bigger raises, which pushes prices up.
Natural rate of unemployment
The normal amount of unemployment when the economy is healthy.
Stagflation
The worst combo: high unemployment and high inflation at the same time.

The output gap and unemployment

Output gap
The percentage difference between actual real GDP and potential output. Negative output gap (recession): the economy isn’t fully using its resources, including labor, so unemployment is above the natural rate. Positive output gap (inflationary): resources are used at higher-than-normal rates, so unemployment is below the natural rate.
Output gap = (actual real GDP − potential output) ÷ potential output × 100

Unemployment moves opposite to the output gap: when the gap is negative, unemployment is above the natural rate.

The short-run Phillips curve

Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC)
The negative short-run relationship between the unemployment rate and the inflation rate. Named for A. W. H. Phillips, who found the pattern in British data.

The AD–AS model and the Phillips curve are the same story

Every point on the Phillips curve matches a point in the AD–AS model. Use this translation table on FRQs:

In AD–ASOn the Phillips curve
AD shifts right (real GDP ↑, PL ↑)Move along the SRPC up and to the left (u ↓, π ↑)
AD shifts left (real GDP ↓, PL ↓)Move along the SRPC down and to the right (u ↑, π ↓)
SRAS shifts left (negative supply shock)SRPC shifts right/up (u ↑ and π ↑: stagflation)
SRAS shifts right (positive supply shock)SRPC shifts left/down (u ↓ and π ↓)

Opposite directions

SRAS and SRPC move in opposite directions. SRAS left → SRPC right. SRAS right → SRPC left. Think of it this way: a bad supply shock makes both unemployment and inflation worse, so the whole curve moves up and out.

Inflation expectations shift the SRPC

Workers and firms build expected inflation into wage contracts and prices. If people expect 2% inflation, nominal wages rise about 2% just to keep up, which pushes prices up 2% even with unemployment at the natural rate.

  • Expected inflation ↑ → SRAS shifts left and SRPC shifts up (right).
  • Expected inflation ↓ → SRAS shifts right and SRPC shifts down (left).

People mostly base their expectations on experience. If inflation has hovered around 0% in recent years, they expect about 0%. If it has averaged 5%, they expect about 5%.

The long-run Phillips curve

Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)
Shows the relationship between unemployment and inflation after expectations have fully adjusted. It is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.
Nonaccelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU)
The unemployment rate at which inflation doesn’t change over time. It equals the natural rate of unemployment.

Why vertical? Say the government holds unemployment below the natural rate. Inflation rises above what people expected. Next year they expect that higher inflation, so the SRPC shifts up, and holding unemployment that low now takes even more inflation. Keep going and you get ever-accelerating inflation. That can’t be sustained, so there is no long-run trade-off between unemployment and inflation.

Shifts of the LRPC

The LRPC is vertical at the natural rate, so only things that change the natural rate of unemployment shift it: changes in labor force characteristics, labor market institutions (like unions or job-matching websites), and government policies (like unemployment benefits or minimum wage).

  • Natural rate ↓ → LRPC shifts left.
  • Natural rate ↑ → LRPC shifts right.

Disinflation and deflation

Disinflation
Bringing the inflation rate down. Because expectations are built into wages, it usually requires a period of higher unemployment (moving down the SRPC) until expectations fall and the SRPC shifts down.
Deflation
A falling aggregate price level (negative inflation). It raises the real burden of debt and can make people delay spending.

U.S. data, 1955–1968 (Figure 5.2-1)

Each dot shows a year’s unemployment rate and the next year’s CPI inflation. Years with low unemployment (like 1966–1968, around 3.5–4%) had higher inflation; years with high unemployment (like 1958 and 1961, around 7%) had low inflation. Data like this were behind the original idea of the Phillips curve.

A recessionary AD shift (Figure 5.2-4)

The economy starts at E₁ at potential output with 2% inflation (the target rate) and unemployment at the natural rate. AD shifts left to AD₂: a negative output gap forms, unemployment rises to 7%, and inflation falls to 0%. The same AD shift shows up as a movement down the SRPC.

Expectations shift the curve (Figure 5.2-6)

With 0% expected inflation, SRPC₀ says inflation will be 0% at 6% unemployment and 2% at 4% unemployment. If expected inflation rises to 2%, the curve shifts up by 2 points at every unemployment rate: SRPC₂ gives 2% inflation at 6% unemployment and 4% at 4% unemployment. At E₀, E₂, and E₄, unemployment is 6% (the natural rate) and actual inflation equals expected inflation.

AP® Econ Tip

Changes in inflationary expectations shift the SRAS curve and the SRPC as a result of their effect on wages. An increase in expected inflation shifts the SRAS curve to the left and the SRPC to the right. A decrease in expected inflation shifts the SRAS curve to the right and the SRPC to the left.

Back to long-run equilibrium (Figure 5.2-9)

If there’s a positive or negative output gap, inflation expectations eventually adjust to the new level of inflation and shift the SRPC so the economy returns to the long-run equilibrium. The lower inflation at point A shifts the SRPC down to SRPC₁ (new equilibrium EA); the higher inflation at point B shifts it up to SRPC₂ (EB). In the long run, the economy returns to the natural rate of unemployment, with inflation set by expectations.

Check yourself

The government increases spending when the economy is at full employment. Show the short-run effect on the Phillips curve.
AD shifts right, so the economy moves along the SRPC up and to the left: unemployment falls below the natural rate and inflation rises.
Oil prices spike. What happens to the SRPC?
SRAS shifts left, raising both unemployment and inflation, so the SRPC shifts right (up).
Why is the LRPC vertical?
In the long run, inflation expectations catch up to actual inflation and the economy returns to the natural rate of unemployment, whatever the inflation rate. Holding unemployment below the natural rate would require ever-accelerating inflation.
A new job-matching website lowers frictional unemployment. Which curve shifts?
The natural rate of unemployment falls, so the LRPC shifts left (and the SRPC shifts left with it).

Module 5.3

Money growth and inflation

In the short run, more money lowers interest rates and raises real GDP. In the long run, nominal wages catch up and the only lasting effect is a higher price level. Money is neutral in the long run, and persistent inflation comes from persistent growth in the money supply.

🧸 Explained simply

Imagine everyone in your class suddenly gets twice as many tickets for the school store. At first, the store sells more stuff. But the store doesn’t have twice as many toys, so it soon doubles every price. Now everyone has twice the tickets, everything costs twice as much, and nobody is actually richer.

That’s money in the long run: more money just means higher prices, not more stuff.

Words, made simple

Monetary neutrality
In the long run, changing how much money there is changes prices, not how much stuff we make.
Velocity of money
How many times a dollar gets spent in a year. You buy lunch, the café pays a worker, the worker buys a book…
Quantity Theory of Money
Money × how fast it’s spent = prices × stuff made. Print more money → prices go up.
Monetarism
The idea that the money supply should grow slowly and steadily, like a set rule, instead of the Fed changing course all the time.

Short run vs. long run effects of a change in the money supply

Short run: an increase in the money supply lowers the interest rate, which raises investment spending and, through the multiplier, consumer spending. AD shifts right, and both real GDP and the price level rise.

MS ↑→i ↓→I ↑→C ↑ (multiplier)→AD ↑→real GDP ↑ and PL ↑

Long run: real GDP is now above potential output, so unemployment is low and nominal wages rise. SRAS shifts left until the economy is back at potential output. Real GDP returns to Y₁, but the price level is even higher.

MS ↑→…→AD ↑→nominal wages ↑, SRAS ←→real GDP back to Yp, PL ↑↑

A decrease in the money supply plays out in reverse: i ↑, I ↓, C ↓, AD ↓, real GDP and PL ↓ in the short run; then nominal wages fall, SRAS shifts right, and real GDP returns to potential with a lower price level.

Monetary neutrality

Monetary neutrality
Changes in the money supply have no real effects on the economy in the long run. The only effect is an equal percentage change in the price level. If the money supply falls 25%, the price level must eventually fall 25%; if it rises 50%, the price level rises 50%. That restores the real quantity of money (M/P) and every other real value to where it started.
Interest rates in the long run

In the short run, more money lowers the interest rate. In the long run, the price level rises proportionally, which raises money demand by the same proportion, so the interest rate goes back to where it started. In the long run, the money supply does not affect the interest rate.

The Quantity Theory of Money

Velocity of money (V)
The number of times the average dollar is spent in a year: V = nominal GDP ÷ money supply.
Quantity Theory of Money
The equation of exchange, plus the assumption that velocity is stable and real GDP is set by potential output in the long run. Then any growth in the money supply shows up as inflation.
M × V = P × Y
M = money supply, V = velocity, P = price level, Y = real GDP, so P × Y = nominal GDP
%ΔM + %ΔV ≈ %ΔP + %ΔY

With V constant (%ΔV = 0) and Y at potential, inflation ≈ money growth − real GDP growth. Money growth faster than output growth causes inflation; persistent high money growth causes persistent high inflation.

Level problems

You can also plug levels into M × V = P × Y. Example: M = $2 trillion, V = 5, real GDP = $8 trillion. Then nominal GDP = 2 × 5 = $10 trillion, and P = 10 ÷ 8 = 1.25 (a price index of 125).

Monetarism

Milton Friedman led a movement called monetarism, which argued that GDP will grow steadily if the money supply grows steadily. Monetarists wanted a monetary policy rule: slow, steady money growth that leaves little discretion to the central bank. The idea depends on velocity being stable, and after 1980 it wasn’t.

From your textbook: Krugman’s Macroeconomics for the AP® Course

Friedman and Schwartz

In A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960 (1963), Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz showed that business cycles had historically been associated with swings in the money supply. Their argument persuaded most economists that monetary policy should play a key role. It also suggested the burden of managing the economy could shift away from fiscal policy, which requires political choices (whose taxes to cut, what to spend on), and toward an independent central bank. Shifting from fiscal to monetary policy makes macroeconomics a more technical, less political issue.

Turkey drops six zeros (2005)

In January 2005, Turkey made 1,000,000 “old” lira equal to 1 “new” lira. Turkish real GDP didn’t change. The only thing that changed was the number of zeros on prices: something that cost 2,000,000 lira now cost 2 lira. That’s monetary neutrality.

Velocity isn’t stable (Figure 5.3-2)

The velocity of M1 (nominal GDP ÷ M1) rose along a smooth, predictable trend from 1960 to 1980, which made monetarists confident. From 1980 to 2008 it moved erratically, and since 2008 it has declined sharply. That’s why a simple money-growth rule fell out of favor.

Check yourself

The money supply grows 10% a year, velocity is constant, and real GDP grows 3%. What is inflation?
%ΔP = 10 + 0 − 3 = 7%.
The Fed doubles the money supply. In the long run, what happens to real GDP, the price level, and the interest rate?
Real GDP: unchanged (back at potential). Price level: doubles. Interest rate: back to its original level.
Why did monetarism lose support after 1980?
Velocity stopped following a stable trend, so steady money growth no longer reliably produced steady growth in nominal GDP.

Module 5.4

Government deficits and the national debt

The budget balance is a flow: the gap between what the government takes in and spends in one year. The national debt is a stock: the total of all past deficits minus past surpluses. Running deficits in a recession can be good policy, but a debt that keeps rising faster than the economy creates real problems.

🧸 Explained simply

Say you get $10 allowance a week but spend $12. You borrow $2 from your parents. That week’s shortfall is your deficit. Do that for 10 weeks and you owe $20. The total you owe is your debt.

Owing a little is OK, especially if you’re growing up and will earn more later. But if you have to borrow just to pay back what you already owe, you get in deeper and deeper.

Words, made simple

Budget surplus
The government collects more than it spends this year.
Budget deficit
The government spends more than it collects this year, so it borrows.
National debt
All the money the government still owes from every year added up.
Debt-to-GDP ratio
How big the debt is compared with how much the whole country makes. Like comparing what you owe to your allowance.
Automatic stabilizers
Rules that kick in by themselves: in bad times taxes fall and help payments rise without anyone voting.
Default
When a borrower stops paying back what it owes.

The budget balance

Budget balance = T − G − TR

T = tax revenue, G = government purchases of goods and services, TR = government transfers. Positive = budget surplus (government saving). Negative = budget deficit.

Other things equal, expansionary fiscal policy (G ↑, TR ↑, T ↓) reduces the budget balance, making a surplus smaller or a deficit bigger. Contractionary fiscal policy increases it.

The business cycle and the cyclically adjusted budget balance

The budget balance also moves on its own over the business cycle, because of automatic stabilizers:

  • In a recession, incomes fall so tax revenue falls, and more people qualify for unemployment benefits and other transfers. The budget moves toward deficit even if no one changes policy.
  • In an expansion, tax revenue rises and transfers fall, so the budget moves toward surplus.
Cyclically adjusted budget balance
An estimate of what the budget balance would be if real GDP were exactly at potential output. It strips out the effect of the business cycle, so it shows whether the government’s policy is adding to deficits over the long run.
Should the budget be balanced every year?

Most economists say no. A balanced-budget rule would force the government to raise taxes or cut spending in a recession (when revenue falls), which is contractionary and would make the recession worse. It would undermine the automatic stabilizers. The goal is a budget that balances on average over the cycle: deficits in bad years, surpluses in good years.

Deficit vs. debt

Budget deficitGovernment (national) debt
TypeFlow, over one yearStock, at a point in time
MeasuresHow much more the government spent than it collected this yearThe total the government currently owes
RelationshipDebt = sum of all past deficits − all past surpluses. A deficit this year adds to the debt; a surplus reduces it.

A note on the U.S. fiscal year: it runs from October 1 to September 30, and is named for the calendar year it ends in.

Problems posed by rising government debt

  • Crowding out: government borrowing competes with private borrowing and raises the real interest rate, reducing private investment (Module 5.5).
  • Opportunity cost of interest payments: money spent on interest can’t be spent on goods and services, transfers, lower taxes, or paying down the debt.
  • A debt spiral: borrowing to pay interest on existing debt pushes the government deeper into debt.
  • Default risk: eventually lenders may question the government’s ability to repay and stop lending. A default is often followed by deep financial and economic turmoil.

Deficits and debt in practice

Debt-to-GDP ratio
Government debt as a percentage of GDP. We use it instead of the raw size of the debt because GDP measures the size of the whole economy, which is a good indicator of the taxes the government could collect. If debt grows more slowly than GDP, the burden of the debt is actually falling compared with potential tax revenue, even if the debt is still rising.
Implicit liabilities
Spending promises that aren’t counted in the official debt but are like debt: mainly Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid. An aging population and rising health care costs make these much more expensive over time.

The National Debt Clock

A digital clock in midtown Manhattan displays the current gross national debt of the United States. On April 29, 2020, it passed $22 trillion.

Japan in the 1990s

Japan used large budget deficits to fight a sluggish economy in the 1990s. It was partly successful: Japan avoided a slump like the 1930s. But running large deficits year after year pushed its government debt to levels that made many observers uneasy.

AP® Econ Tip

Government debt, sometimes called the national debt, is equal to the sum of all of the government’s past annual deficits and surpluses. A budget deficit or surplus is for a single budget year. The national debt is the accumulated total amount the government currently owes.

The debt-to-GDP ratio after World War II (Figure 5.4-3)

Federal debt grew in almost every year, yet the debt-to-GDP ratio fell for 30 years after World War II, because GDP grew faster than debt. Growth and inflation can let a government running persistent deficits still have a declining debt-to-GDP ratio.

2008 and 2020

When large deficits aren’t offset by growth, the ratio rises. During 2008–2010, bank bailouts and expansionary spending and tax policies during slow growth pushed the ratio up. In 2020, several coronavirus relief bills produced a federal deficit of $3.1 trillion, more than triple the 2019 deficit.

Future demands on the budget (Figure 5.4-4)

Spending on social insurance programs is projected to rise sharply as a share of GDP, partly from an aging population but mainly because of rising health care costs.

Module 5.5

Crowding out

To finance a deficit, the government borrows by selling bonds. That raises the demand for loanable funds and the real interest rate, which reduces private investment. Less investment today means less physical capital tomorrow, and slower long-run growth.

🧸 Explained simply

There’s only so much money people are willing to lend. If the government shows up and borrows a big chunk, lenders can charge more (higher interest), and some businesses decide it’s too expensive to borrow for new machines. The government crowded out the businesses, like a big kid taking up most of the slide.

Fewer new machines today means the country grows a bit slower later.

Words, made simple

Loanable funds market
The pretend marketplace where savers lend and borrowers borrow. The price is the real interest rate.
Crowding out
Government borrowing pushes interest rates up, so businesses borrow and build less.
Physical capital
Machines, tools, and buildings used to make things.
Treasury bonds
IOUs the government sells to borrow money.

How the government finances its debt

When a government spends more than it collects, it borrows, usually by selling bonds. In the U.S., the Treasury Department issues Treasury bills, notes, and bonds. In the United Kingdom, the Debt Management Office (sponsored by HM Treasury) sells government bonds, bills, and gilts (a type of bond). Every bond means future interest payments.

Crowding out in the loanable funds market

Deficit ↑→government borrowing ↑→DLF →→real r ↑→private I ↓→less physical capital→slower long-run growth
Graph check

Crowding out is shown on the loanable funds graph with the real interest rate on the vertical axis, not on the money market graph. Some teachers instead show a deficit as a leftward shift of supply (less public saving). Either way, the real interest rate rises and private investment falls. Use whichever version your teacher uses.

The flip side

A budget surplus does the reverse: less government borrowing (or the government supplying funds by paying down debt) lowers the real interest rate and encourages private investment, adding to long-run growth.

When crowding out is small

In a deep recession with lots of idle savings and low interest rates, extra government borrowing raises interest rates very little, so crowding out is small. It matters most when the economy is near full employment.

From your textbook: Krugman’s Macroeconomics for the AP® Course

Financing the debt

In the United Kingdom, government debt is financed through the Debt Management Office, an agency sponsored by HM Treasury, which sells government bonds, bills, and gilts. In the United States, the Treasury Department plays that role.

Why a higher real interest rate matters

Beyond the opportunity cost of interest payments, persistent deficits and rising debt can raise real interest rates in the loanable funds market by increasing the demand for loanable funds. The real interest rate is a key determinant of investment: a higher rate makes borrowing more expensive and reduces business investment, while a lower rate makes borrowing cheaper and encourages it.

Check yourself

The government increases its deficit. Show and explain crowding out.
Demand for loanable funds shifts right as the government borrows more. The real interest rate rises, and private investment spending falls.
How can crowding out hurt long-run growth?
Less private investment means a smaller stock of physical capital, so productivity and potential output grow more slowly. LRAS (and the PPC) shift out less.
Which interest rate rises in crowding out, real or nominal, and on which graph?
The real interest rate, on the loanable funds graph.

Module 5.6

Economic growth

Long-run economic growth is a sustained rise in real GDP per capita. It comes from rising productivity: more output per worker, driven by physical capital, human capital, and technology. Growth shows up as an outward shift of the PPC and a rightward shift of LRAS.

🧸 Explained simply

A country gets richer when each person can make more stuff. Three things help: better tools (physical capital), more skills (human capital), and smarter ways of doing things (technology).

A worker with a shovel digs a little. A worker with a backhoe digs a lot. Teach her to drive it well and use a better plan, and she digs even more.

Words, made simple

Real GDP per capita
All the stuff a country makes, divided by the number of people. The best simple measure of how well people live.
Productivity
How much one worker makes in an hour or a year.
Physical capital
Tools, machines, buildings.
Human capital
Skills and knowledge people have from school and practice.
Technology
Better ways of making things, from computer chips to Post-it notes.
Rule of 70
A shortcut: 70 ÷ growth rate = how many years until something doubles.

Measuring growth

Real GDP per capita = real GDP ÷ population
Growth rate = (this year − last year) ÷ last year × 100
Years to double ≈ 70 ÷ annual growth rate

We use real GDP to remove the effect of rising prices and per capita to remove the effect of population growth. If real GDP grows only as fast as population, real GDP per capita doesn’t change and the standard of living doesn’t rise.

The sources of long-run growth: productivity

Labor productivity
Output per worker (or per hour worked) = real GDP ÷ number of workers. Sustained growth in real GDP per capita happens only when productivity rises.
Physical capital
Human-made resources such as buildings and machinery that make workers more productive.
Human capital
The improvement in labor created by the education and knowledge embodied in the workforce.
Technology
The technical means for producing goods and services. Technological progress lets workers produce more with the same physical and human capital.

The aggregate production function

Real GDP = f(K, L)

K = the stock of physical capital; L = the quantity and quality (human capital) of labor. “f” means “is a function of,” or “depends on.” Technology determines the shape of f.

Diminishing returns to physical capital
Holding human capital and technology fixed, each additional unit of physical capital per worker raises productivity by less than the one before. The first backhoe makes a huge difference; the fifth backhoe for the same worker adds almost nothing. That’s why the production function is drawn bowed downward (concave). Technological progress shifts the whole curve up.

Growth in the models

ModelHow long-run growth is shown
PPCOutward shift of the whole curve. A country that produces more investment (capital) goods today shifts its PPC out further tomorrow.
AD–ASRightward shift of LRAS (potential output increases)
Phillips curveNo direct effect, unless the natural rate of unemployment changes
Long-run growth vs. short-run fluctuations

Short-run changes in real GDP come from AD or SRAS shifts (the business cycle). Long-run growth is an increase in potential output: LRAS shifts right. An increase in AD alone can’t create long-run growth.

Why per capita

Other things equal, an increase in population lowers the standard of living for the average person, since more people share a given amount of real GDP. Shovel vs. backhoe

A worker operating a backhoe can dig a lot more feet of trench per day than one with only a shovel. That’s physical capital raising productivity.

AP® Econ Tip

Be careful when describing what leads to long-run growth. “Increased investment in physical capital” leads to long-run economic growth because capital accumulation raises productivity. “Increased investment” alone describes a short-run change in aggregate demand, because investment is a component of AD.

Modest inventions matter

Economically important technology doesn’t have to be flashy. Growth has been driven by big inventions like the railroad and the semiconductor chip, but also by thousands of modest ones. The flat-bottomed paper bag (patented 1870) made packing groceries much easier, and the Post-it note (1981) had surprisingly large benefits for office productivity.

Land in the production function

In theory, land affects output too, but in modern economies it matters far less than physical capital and labor, which is why the production function is written as f(K, L). In some less developed economies, land and livestock are still important inputs.

Kyland’s PPC

Your book’s example country, Kyland, chooses between consumer goods (food, clothing, sporting goods) and investment goods (physical capital used to produce other goods). The PPC is bowed out because of increasing opportunity cost. Choosing more investment goods today (point A or B instead of C) means faster growth in the future.

AP® Econ Tip

The LRAS curve is vertical at the economy’s potential output (full employment output), which corresponds to the natural rate of unemployment. Economic growth is shown in the AD–AS model by a rightward shift of LRAS. Long-run growth is distinct from short-run fluctuations, which are shown by changes in short-run equilibrium due to shifts in SRAS or AD.

Module 5.7

Public policy and economic growth

Countries grow at very different rates because they differ in how much they save and invest, how fast they build human capital, and how much research and development they do. Governments can promote growth through infrastructure, education, a sound financial system, protection of property rights, R&D, and supply-side policies.

🧸 Explained simply

Some countries get richer faster than others. The fast ones usually save and build a lot, send lots of kids to school, and invent and use new ideas. The government can help by building roads and power lines, running good schools, keeping banks trustworthy, and making sure people get to keep what they build.

Words, made simple

Infrastructure
Stuff everyone uses: roads, power lines, internet, clean water.
R&D
Research and development: inventing new things and making them work in real life.
Property rights
Rules that say what’s yours is yours, so people bother to build things.
Supply-side policies
Government changes that make people want to work, save, and build more, like lower tax rates.
Laffer curve
The idea that if taxes are super high, cutting them could even bring in more money. Economists argue about whether that’s true in real life.

Why growth rates differ

Savings and investment spending
Countries that grow fast usually invest a large share of GDP in physical capital. Foreign funds have helped some countries (the U.S. relied heavily on them during early industrialization), but mostly, countries invest a lot because they have high domestic savings.
Human capital
Educational levels rose much faster in fast-growing countries.
Research and development (R&D)
Spending to create new technologies and prepare them for practical use. Developing new technology is one thing; adopting it is another, and countries differ in how fast they apply new technologies.

Supply-side fiscal policies

Supply-side policies try to raise the incentive to work, save, and invest, for example by lowering tax rates or reducing regulation. They have the potential to shift both SRAS and LRAS to the right, raising potential output.

Laffer curve
A hypothetical curve showing that tax revenue first rises and then falls as the tax rate goes from 0% to 100%. On the downward part, cutting the tax rate would raise revenue. Almost all economists agree that tax cuts raise incentives to work, save, and invest. They don’t agree that tax cuts will raise tax revenue or sharply raise potential output, because it isn’t clear that real tax rates are high enough to be on that part of the curve.

Growth and the environment

More production has usually meant more pollution and carbon emissions, but slowing growth isn’t the only way to address climate change. Policies like emissions taxes, tradable permits, and support for green technology can reconcile long-run growth with environmental protection; the hard part is getting political agreement, including among countries.

Argentina vs. China (Figure 5.7-1)

Seventy years ago, Argentina’s population was much more educated than China’s, where much of the population was illiterate. Education has risen in both countries, but much faster in China. China’s average is still slightly below Argentina’s, mainly because many elderly adults never received basic education, but in high school and college education China has outstripped once-rich Argentina.

Edison invented R&D

In 1875, Thomas Edison created his Menlo Park, New Jersey, laboratory, employing 25 men full time to generate new products and processes year after year. Today R&D operations are standard throughout business. Edison’s R&D was paid for by the private sector, which is still common, but some R&D is done by governments. The U.S. became the world’s leading economy largely because American businesses were among the first to make R&D a systematic part of their operations.

Applying technology

America’s surge in productivity growth after 1995, as firms learned to use information technology, was not matched in Europe at first.

Ireland’s telecommunications

Ireland’s economy took off in the 1990s. The government had invested in excellent telecommunications infrastructure in the 1980s, which helped make Ireland a favored location for high-tech companies. Poor infrastructure, like a power grid that often fails, is a major obstacle in some countries. Basic public health (clean water, disease control) may be the most crucial infrastructure of all, especially in low-income countries in Africa. Projects like a hydropower station in Angola (inaugurated in 2017) are key to growth there.

Trust in banks

If people trust their banks, they deposit savings, and banks lend them to businesses. If they don’t, they hoard gold or foreign currency under the mattress, where it can’t become productive investment. A well-functioning financial system requires appropriate government regulation.

COVID-19 and emissions

Reduced economic activity during the pandemic led to a 4.9% decrease in global GDP in the second quarter of 2020, and to a drop in carbon emissions. The U.S., the European Union, China, and India produce almost 60% of human-caused carbon emissions. As economies rebounded, emissions did too. The positive link between production and emissions doesn’t mean shrinking the economy is the only way to fight climate change.

Name a government policy that raises each source of productivity: physical capital, human capital, technology.
Physical capital: investment tax credits or public infrastructure. Human capital: public education or subsidized college. Technology: funding research or strong patent protection.
What are supply-side policies, and how would you show their effect in AD–AS?
Policies that raise incentives to work, save, and invest, such as lower marginal tax rates. They shift SRAS and LRAS to the right.
Why does a trustworthy banking system matter for growth?
It channels savings into business investment. Without trust, people hoard savings instead, and the economy builds less physical capital.
